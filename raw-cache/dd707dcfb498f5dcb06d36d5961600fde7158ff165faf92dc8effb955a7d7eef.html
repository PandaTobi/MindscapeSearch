The greatness or the lack of greatness is less interesting to me than what was actually accomplished.

3:37:12.6 SC: Well, the good news is Laplace's demon doesn't care about those things, so their mental health is not in any danger. But your intuition is correct. Laplace's demon... That's completely allowed. But Laplace's demon doesn't exist, so don't set up any GoFundMe pages. If you do see any out there, they're probably just scams.

3:38:04.5 SC: Alex Dubrow says, "In your recent conversation with Chandra Sripada, you said his arguments shifted your credences about whether LLMs have rediscovered some of the ways humans think. I'm curious where your credences are now. Given your views on emergence and levels of description, to what extent do you think that today's most capable LLMs exhibit intelligence or cognition in something like the same sense that humans do? 3:38:56.8 SC: Well, the thing at the end about prediction shaping both systems is exactly Chandra's point. That's what he wants to hypothesize is actually happening here. But I want to separate this issue from the issue of silicon versus biology, okay? That may or may not become important to questions of consciousness, and we'll get to that in the very next question. But cognition was what Chandra was thinking about. And cognition is much simpler in some sense. It's just reasoning, just thinking, just adding numbers together, making decisions, things like that. It's not conscious experience or any such thing.

3:39:34.4 SC: So the question with LLMs always was the following. They're not built, they're not architected or designed to mimic the actual structures in the human brain, okay? They don't have a cerebellum and frontal cortex and all that stuff. Their architecture is inspired by the idea of neurons and how they talk to each other, but they're not wired together in some hierarchical way relative to what the human brain is doing. Instead, they are trained to give outputs based on inputs that resemble what human beings would do. And so the open question always is, did LLMs manage to accurately mimic what human beings would do by rediscovering the way human beings do them, or by discovering a new way to do really similar things? And there's good arguments on both sides, right? Some arguments for the similarities come from just the similarities of the inputs and the outputs. Some arguments for the differences come from the fact that when LLMs do make mistakes, there's sort of a characteristic kind of mistake that they make that human beings wouldn't make, that indicates that they're reasoning differently in some ways. And so I think it's an interesting open question. And I've often said this, and people said, "Oh no, they clearly have rediscovered human ways of thinking," but all of their evidence was just... They sound human at the end of the day. That's what it always came down to, or they're really smart. But it was the kind of smartness that was under question, not the existence of their smartness. And I thought that Chandra gave a good job of at least making an argument that we have evidence that the way in which they're thinking is closely related to that of humans. Now, others strongly disagree with this, right? This is not a consensus view by any stretch. There is no consensus view about this, but I was glad to see some real evidence put forward. I think that it's just the beginning of us understanding this. I don't think that we're close to figuring out once and for all the similarities or differences between humans and LLMs.

3:41:44.9 SC: As Chandra readily admits, you need a lot more input, you need a lot more tokens to train your LLM to give those human-like responses. And that's kind of obvious why. It's namely because human brains have structure in them that LLMs are not born with. So that's an important difference. So I don't think it's... All of that discussion is completely aside from the question of silicon versus biology or anything like that. But it's very, very interesting to imagine that the way that we human beings go about cogitating, as optimized by natural selection and the ability, the desire to make accurate predictions, turns out via convergent evolution to be similar to the way that LLMs go about reasoning. I don't have strong feelings one way or the other, but I'm absolutely open to the evidence pushing us in one direction or the other.

3:42:40.0 SC: Then Josh says that, "Chandra said you used to lean functionalist about consciousness, but you might have drifted away from it. Where do you actually stand now? Did his mechanistic case pull you back at all, or are you still leaning towards something like Anil Seth's biological naturalism?"

3:42:56.6 SC: So I'm on neither side. So number one, Chandra was not talking about consciousness. We were not talking about consciousness in that podcast. We were talking about cognition, which is something different. And number two, Anil Seth and also Ned Block, we recently had on the podcast, have made cases against computational functionalism as a way of thinking about consciousness without giving up on physicalism about consciousness. So again, I'm not pretending to be an expert on consciousness. I really promise you I am not. And part of my non-expertness was that I never quite appreciated that you could be a physicalist without being a computational functionalist. But there is a leap involved, and I think that Anil and Ned are both completely correct about this, that just because at the end of the day there's physical stuff moving around in different ways, obeying the laws of physics, that's not enough to make you instantly leap to thinking that whatever consciousness is, it's a matter of functional processing of information. There might be more to it than that, even if the more is still 100% physical, not weird, spooky, ineffable, spiritual stuff, right? So I think that's all. I think that's the only change that has happened in my conception of consciousness is that I have a greater appreciation for the difference conceptually in saying that you're a physicalist and saying you're a computational functionalist. So I'm open to the possibility that there are things going on which don't map on in any direct way to computation, but are going on in the human brain or human biology, just in the metabolic processes, the entropic gradients, whatever is going on in the brain that doesn't come across in a computational description, but is nevertheless important for consciousness. I'm open to that possibility. I'm very open to lots of things when it comes to consciousness as long as they're compatible with the laws of physics.

3:45:04.1 SC: Brett Slog says, "What do you make of the growing movement to oppose the construction of data centers? There seems to be some elements of a moral panic, particularly around the idea they consume large amounts of water, mixed with some semi-legitimate NIMBY-esque objections about noise and aesthetics, all clouded by general anxiety about the future of AI."

3:45:24.9 SC: Yeah, I think that your reading is pretty similar to my own. I don't have strong opinions here one way or the other. It reminds me of a question we got a couple months ago in AMA about my feeling about the lab leak hypothesis for the origin of COVID-19. And I said, look, I don't care in some ways. That's not the case with data centers. For data centers, I care. For COVID, I didn't care. I don't care if it's natural origins or synthetic origins. What I care about is being honest and scientific about getting the answer. And it became very difficult to do that because people have so many non-epistemic values and interests that come into the discussion of these issues. And so I think the same thing is true with data centers. There's, on the one hand, there's an argument to be made that data centers are useful. On the other hand, there are absolutely legitimate arguments that they cause harm if they're done badly, right? That they cause pollution and that they're a waste of money and they run rampant over people's rights and lands and all these things. And it's just very, very difficult to have that conversation because the pro-data center side is entirely biased by money. They want to make money. They're going to tell you the data centers are good because if they build one, they'll make billions of dollars. And so it's kind of hard to take them at face value when they make those arguments. But the anti-data center side is also contaminated by semi-Luddite objections to the very ideas of technology and capitalism or whatever. And I think you need a nuanced discussion, and it's just hard to find nuanced discussions. So I don't dig very much into that myself. I don't get my hackles up about data centers pro or con.

3:47:16.5 SC: The one thing, I guess, that really does kind of rub me the wrong way is the gigantic imbalance of power between rich people and non-rich people in the country, right? If I had to pick a side, I would be, given that I'm not very up on the actual issues here, I would be predisposed to pick the side of the people who are not billionaires and trillionaires because their voices are harder to get heard, right? And so I would think that if you just listened to the voices out there, you're going to get a biased view in a very particular direction. But that's no way to make difficult decisions about public policy. So I think that it's weird to see, for example, the Democratic Party, which in many ways you might think would be anti-data center. And why would you think that? Well, number one, they want to preserve the environment. Number two, they want to oppose Republicans who are very pro-data center. And number three, data centers are really unpopular right now. So you might think, wow, this is a real obvious winner for Democratic politicians. And then you look at the actual positions of Democratic politicians, and many of them are very pro-data center. And you go, oh, my goodness, what's happening? Guess what's happening. They're getting lots of money in their campaign contributions from the companies who want to build data centers. So that huge amount of money, they just distort the discussion, the public dialogue around these things by quite a bit. And that does absolutely rub me the wrong way as much as any specific issue about using up water or whatever for the data centers.
