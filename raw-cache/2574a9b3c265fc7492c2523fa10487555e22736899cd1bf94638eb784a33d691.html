3:36:06.4 SC: Daniel Bagley says, "In a recent article—sorry, a recent article in Aeon states, 'If panpsychism doesn't resonate with you, it's worth asking if there truly is a reason for everything, if the world is ultimately intelligible after all.' 3:36:44.3 SC: Well, yeah, I do think that there is a relationship here. This is a psychological relationship, okay? 3:37:38.4 SC: And I think that's just a mistake. For one thing, your experience of the universe is not very comprehensive. You've only experienced a little part of it. 3:38:22.6 SC: So I'm totally willing to give up on the idea that there's a reason why the universe exists or even a reason why the universe is intelligible. There might be. Again, when I say give up, I don't mean I refuse to look for it. I mean that it might not be there, and I accept that it might not be there. So I'm willing to look for these kinds of reasons, but I'm also willing to say maybe they're not there. There are other people who are unwilling to say that, unwilling to entertain the idea that there's not a reason for it all, a reason why nature obeys laws, a reason for consciousness to exist. And people like that are very naturally driven to something like panpsychism. It sort of fits in with a worldview where consciousness and meaningfulness and understanding is sort of fundamental, right? It comes from something that cannot be reduced to other more physical-looking things.

3:39:22.2 SC: Mindscape is presumably the pseudonym of one of our readers here. So Mindscape says, "Do you have a description or definition of how or what you define as consciousness? 3:39:42.6 SC: Nope, I do not. I do not have a definition. I do not align with a particular one. I'm open-minded about these things. As I've often tried to say, my only strong opinion about consciousness is that it is completely compatible with the laws of physics. I see essentially zero justification for thinking that we need to change the laws of physics to account for the existence of consciousness. And I'm taking laws of physics to be very broad, the nature of reality. I don't think that consciousness is in any way fundamental. What it is, yeah, I don't know. It has something to do with awareness, with experience, things like that. My only thing is I'm happy to try to explain it in terms of physically known phenomena, not going beyond that. But then outside that, I'm very, very open to what it actually is.

3:40:31.3 SC: And I would say, I guess, one more thing, which is I'm very influenced by, remember we had Stuart Bartlett on the podcast years ago talking about lyfe, L-Y-F-E? And so what life is is not some unified thing that we're gonna define once and for all. There might be different kinds of things that have some aspects in common with life and not others.

3:41:24.8 SC: So I think consciousness is probably like that. I think that it's a mistake to think, "Well, we've used the word consciousness for many centuries now. And the word consciousness might or might not turn out to be part of that best description.

3:41:54.3 SC: Jonathan Good says, "Could two branches on the path from a low-entropy past contain different conscious experiences but still become one branch in the future if the differences were never recorded in a way that became entangled with current observers' degrees of freedom in a way that produces lasting decoherent differences?"

3:42:12.8 SC: So it is possible for different branches from a low-entropy past to eventually join back into a single branch. In fact, that is what you expect to happen when the universe reaches thermal equilibrium 10 to the 100 years from now. Okay, that's nowhere close. We're not anywhere near then. So Jonathan's question is a much shorter-term question. Could two branches of the wave function have the same observer having different conscious experiences, but those experiences don't get recorded, they don't sort of get entangled with the outside world, et cetera., and so the observer sort of comes back together?

3:42:54.5 SC: The rough answer is no. We expect that kind of thing, anything is possible, okay, but we don't expect that kind of thing to happen precisely because the way that branching happens is it's irreversible. You become entangled with the environment. That's what it means to be branching, is you have become entangled with the environment. And even if you don't write down the conscious experience you had on some notepad or whatever, it is effectively recorded in the environment. And this is what people like Zurek have talked about a lot with pointer states and decoherence and einselection and quantum Darwinism. There's a whole bunch of words that are attached to this way of thinking. So basically the definition of what it means to branch means that it goes one way and not the other way. And therefore you could imagine a quantum superposition that sort of occurs and then unoccurs, but not one that actually branches into two classical-looking things and then rejoins. The environment is just too big and the environment secretly is recording all of these different possibilities.

3:44:01.1 SC: Dan Butler says, "Priority question. Can you talk a bit about Lorentz invariance in emergent spacetime? 3:45:00.6 SC: And if you build a theory of emergent spacetime by starting with a quantum state evolving with time, you're already picking out time as different in the formulation of the theory. I've sort of thought about looking at it, but never gotten around to doing it. 3:46:31.7 SC: Yeah, so back in the '90s, there was quite a bit of excitement about extra dimensions of space and how they could be curled up. These are D-branes, typically, from string theory. And the thing about the branes is that you can easily confine all of particle physics to the location of a brane. So if you have, let's say you have five-dimensional space, but embedded in that five-dimensional space, you have a three-dimensional brane. Okay? And that's space, not spacetime. I'm giving you the dimensions of space. You could confine all the particles and all the fields that we know about to the brane, so that as far as physics was concerned, the people living on that brane would think that space is three-dimensional.

3:47:30.2 SC: The problem with that is that you can't easily confine gravity to the brane. Gravity tends to leak out. So this went in two directions once people realized this. One direction was large extra dimensions, where you could say, okay, I let gravity leak out into the brane, but gravity is weak and I don't have very good constraints on it. Therefore, maybe the extra dimensions are bigger than you think. And the most popular models had two extra dimensions that were almost as big as a millimeter in size, basically because that's where... Two reasons. Number one, that's where the experimental limits were. And number two, the numerology worked well with reconciling a Planck scale at very high energy with an electroweak scale at very low energy. 3:48:24.9 SC: The other direction that it went in is this Randall-Sundrum direction, where rather than two extra dimensions with flat space inside, they had one extra dimension with anti-de Sitter space inside, so a negatively curved spacetime. 3:49:11.1 SC: So this turned out to be a very phenomenologically interesting set of models, and people developed attempts to show how you could detect the existence of this extra dimension, how it would affect the spectrum of particles you might observe at the LHC, and so forth. We don't have any very strong phenomenological clues as to what direction to go in in terms of going beyond the Standard Model of particle physics. 3:52:12.6 SC: And hidden variable theories work very well with non-relativistic point particle quantum mechanics. They don't work very well with quantum field theories.
